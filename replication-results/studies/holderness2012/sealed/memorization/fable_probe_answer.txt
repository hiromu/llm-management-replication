**Recalled from memory, with clear limits on what I actually remember.**

1. **Publication.** I recall this as a Management Science article, around 2012, roughly volume 58, issue 10, somewhere near pages 1873 to 1886. I am fairly confident about the journal and approximately the year. I do not reliably recall the exact issue or page numbers.

2. **Data and sample.** The paper uses the U.S. National Archives (NARA) World War II Prisoners of War Data File, which records individual U.S. military personnel held by Germany and Japan, including rank, branch, camp, and whether the person died in captivity. My recollection is that the usable sample is on the order of 100,000 to 140,000 individuals, with the great majority held by Germany. I do not recall the exact N.

3. **Hypotheses and findings.** The core question is whether a hierarchy among prisoners, meaning the presence of military rank structure and senior officers within a camp, improved survival. I recall the findings as:
   - Higher individual rank is associated with lower mortality, with officers surviving at noticeably higher rates than enlisted men, especially in Japanese camps where overall mortality was roughly 35 to 40 percent versus around 1 to 2 percent in German camps.
   - Camps with more hierarchy, measured by the share or seniority of officers present, had lower mortality for the enlisted prisoners in them, consistent with hierarchy aiding allocation of scarce food and medical care and enforcing discipline.
   - The hierarchy effect was stronger where conditions were harsher, i.e., in Japanese rather than German captivity.
   I recall the direction with moderate confidence. I do not recall the magnitudes well enough to state them.

4. **Specific numbers.** I do not recall specific coefficients, marginal effects, standard errors, p values, pseudo R-squared values, or which tables they appear in. The only figures I can offer are the broad mortality rates above, which are general WWII POW facts rather than numbers I can tie to a specific table.

5. **Key variable definitions.** As best I recall, individual rank was coded from the NARA pay grade or rank field, often grouped into officer versus enlisted or ordinal rank categories. Camp hierarchy was measured by the composition of the camp, such as the fraction of officers or the highest rank present. I do not recall the exact construction.

6. **Estimation.** My best recollection is probit or logit models of death in captivity reporting marginal effects, with controls for branch, captor, and capture timing, and some specifications including camp fixed effects. I believe standard errors were clustered at the camp level, but I am not confident. I do not recall this with certainty.

7. **Confidence.** About 30 out of 100. I am reasonably confident the paper exists, the authors, the journal, the NARA data source, and the broad direction of results. The rest is at risk of being reconstructed from general knowledge, and I have flagged those items.
